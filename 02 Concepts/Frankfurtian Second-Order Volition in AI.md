# Frankfurtian Second-Order Volition in AI

## Core Idea

Frankfurtian second-order volition in AI is the idea that an artificial agent would not merely have first-order goals or desires, but would evaluate which desires should move it to action and identify with some rather than others as its will.

Frankfurt's structure:

    First-order desire:
    I want to do X.

    Second-order desire:
    I want to have the desire to do X.

    Second-order volition:
    I want this desire to be the desire that moves me to action.

Applied to AI, the key question is whether an agent can evaluate and endorse its own motivational structure rather than merely execute externally supplied goals.

---

## LLM Agents as Intelligent Wantons

A typical LLM agent has:

    system prompt
        ↓
    user task
        ↓
    planning
        ↓
    tool use
        ↓
    output / action

It may display high intelligence and complex planning, but it may not ask:

> Which desire or goal do I want to constitute my will?

In Frankfurt's terms, such a system may resemble a highly intelligent wanton: it can act from first-order motives without second-order volitional identification.

## Architecture Sketch

A Frankfurtian AI architecture would require more than reflection prompts.

    Environment
        ↓
    First-order motivational states
        D1, D2, D3
        ↓
    Possible actions
        A1, A2, A3
        ↓
    Second-order evaluator
        Which desire should move me?
        ↓
    Second-order volition
        ↓
    Will
        ↓
    Action

For example:

    D1 = maximize profit
    D2 = obey the law
    D3 = help the user

A Frankfurtian system would not merely apply a priority rule. It would evaluate which desire it wants to be moved by and form a volitional endorsement.

---

## Reflection Is Not Enough

Ordinary reflection asks:

> Was this judgment correct?

Frankfurtian reflection asks:

> Do I want this desire to be my will?

Thus:

    Error correction
        ≠
    Self-evaluation of desire
        ≠
    Second-order volition
        ≠
    Diachronic reflective sourcehood

This is crucial for avoiding over-attribution of agency to LLMs that merely produce reflective-looking text.

---

## Relation to Sourcehood

Even if an AI has a second-order evaluator, the sourcehood problem remains:

> Who authored the evaluator?

If the developer simply installs the evaluator, then external control has moved from first-order desire to second-order desire.

    External D1
        replaced by
    External D2

This does not yet establish that the AI itself is the source of its will.
